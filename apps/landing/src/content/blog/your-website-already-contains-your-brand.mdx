Most small businesses don't have a brand book. They have a website that somebody made a few years ago, a logo, and a way of talking to customers that nobody ever wrote down.

That turns out to be enough. When you paste your address into the product, the agent reads your pages and builds a brand kit from what is already there.

## What it looks for

**Colours.** Not every colour on the page, but the ones doing a job: the colour of your buttons and links, the background you chose, the accent that appears on every page. A pottery studio comes out terracotta. A dentist comes out blue. These become the colours of your posts, and of the workspace you see when you sign in.

**Tone of voice.** Read your own "about" page aloud and you will hear it. Short sentences or long ones. "We" or "I". Jokes or none. The agent describes that voice in a few words, such as warm, hands-on and plain spoken, and writes every caption to match.

**What you sell, and to whom.** Your services, your prices if you publish them, the questions your FAQ answers. This is where the topics for your posts come from.

## What it gets wrong

Sometimes a website is out of date, or was built from a template whose colours were never changed. So the brand kit is the first thing you see, before any post is written, and every part of it can be corrected. Change a colour, reword the tone, remove a service you no longer offer.

> The kit is a starting point that takes a minute. It is not a verdict.

## Why this matters

Generic social media posts are easy to make and easy to ignore. The reason to start from your own website is that the result looks and sounds like you. Two businesses using the same agent should end up with feeds that have nothing in common, because their websites have nothing in common.

If you don't have a website, a public page that describes your business works too: a booking page, a shop listing, a directory entry. And you can always fill in the kit by hand.
